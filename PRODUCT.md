# Tempo product definition

## Purpose

Tempo answers four practical questions: what to practice today, how today’s practice went, how training is changing over time, and which skill belongs next. It should feel like following a thoughtful personal program, never like maintaining a spreadsheet or chasing a score.

The primary flow is intentionally short:

> Open → Today’s Session → practice one exercise at a time → record a quick result → finish → review progress.

## Training philosophy

Control, timing, consistency, and relaxation matter more than speed. Tempo changes one meaningful variable at a time and never promotes BPM as a proxy for musicianship. A missed day has no punitive language, a recovery pulse is good technique, and exploratory material can remain “Developing” without making a session a failure.

When an exercise becomes difficult:

1. Slow down.
2. Shorten the pattern.
3. Isolate the problem.
4. Count aloud.
5. Rebuild the pattern.

## Current stage

The active phase is **Foundation**, practiced on a pad with 5A sticks in 30–45 minute sessions.

- 60 BPM is the solid foundation tempo.
- 65 BPM is the current working tempo.
- 70 BPM is an optional challenge tempo.
- Approximately 50 BPM introduces short sixteenth-note bursts.

Current work covers quarters, eighths, singles, doubles, lead changes, subdivision transitions, play/rest/return, internal timing, early sixteenths, and very light optional foot coordination.

## Roadmap boundaries

Upcoming skills—accents, paradiddles, triplets, rests, basic reading, and additional foot coordination—remain visible but inactive. Kit-specific work such as grooves, fills, dynamics, kick coordination, and song practice belongs to a later phase.

Tempo is not a generic lesson marketplace, AI coach, competitive game, social network, or detailed audio workstation. It does not fabricate coaching or historical performance. The scope is a private structured practice tracker and session runner.
